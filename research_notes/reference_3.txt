Vehicle AC Compressor’s Piston Defect Classiﬁcation with DCGAN-Enhanced CNN Dedi Arianto and Nughthoh Arfawi Kurdhi Abstract Industrial defect detection has traditionally been done using human eyesight, which has always been costly, subjective, and often inaccurate. This under-scores the importance of automated techniques like computer vision for effective quality control. We propose to incorporate Deep Convolutional Generative Adver-sarial Network (DCGAN) as a data approach for the augmentation of other computer vision methodologies, speciﬁcally Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), in cate-gorizing defective pistons images. Our approach deals with issues associated with limited and unequal distributions by creating artiﬁcially-induced defects in the training set. We categorized our piston images into three groups: Normal, Defected 1 (broken, shaped out, and fallen), and Defected 2 (rust, oil, and grease marks). The results indicated that employing synthetic data generated by DCGAN improves CNN’s F1-score tremendously from the classical dataset’s 0.7750 to 0.9386. The F1-score for the combined datasets, consisting of synthetic and classical datasets, was equal to 0.8959. These ﬁndings demonstrate that DCGAN enhances overall accuracy for defect detection in the manufacturing industry, thereby providing them with reliable solutions that reduce the need for manual labeling processes. The recent advancements in deep learning have enhanced the industrial defect detection with a ﬁrm hand, with CNNs performing their best for tasks like classiﬁ-cation and segmentation. For instance, Song et al. further designed the CSGNet with YOLOv6 framework on aero-engine blades for defect detection based on advanced data augmentation and optimization techniques [3], whereas Huang et al. used R-CNN with engine parts for defect detection, resulting in faster detection and higher mAP on engine parts detection [4]. Additionally, Principi et al. demonstrated that deep autoencoders are able to efﬁciently detect electric motor faults compared with conventional approaches [5]. While deep learning has greatly improved over the years, its application in indus-trial defect detection has been very challenging because obtaining enough defect data is challenging, especially for imbalanced datasets where model performances could be less reliable [6]. The increasing complexities of deep learning models consequently require more diverse and balanced datasets. Nevertheless, it has gener-ally been costly and time-consuming to collect such large amounts of data with accurate labels [7]. These challenges can be mitigated by applying data augmenta-tion techniques, including image translation, rotation, ﬂipping, and scaling, though they usually offer little added beneﬁt [8]. A step ahead in this line is the synthetic data augmentation using generative models like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), inspired by game theory, a very promising method to generate fake realistic images for improving the model training [9]. In this paper, we propose a type of data augmentation with the help of Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Networks (DCGAN). Our original use case includes the factory dataset, which was collected manually from a component by rotating it 360 degrees in order to collect disparate angles of images and cover all levels of detail. With DCGAN, we can generate synthetic defect samples, which allows us to rebalance our rather inconveniently scarce and imbalanced training dataset. Then, these augmented datasets will be used as input in order to classify three working conditions, which are Normal, Defected 1 (broken, shaped out, and fell), and Defected 2 (stain with oil, grease, and rust) by using the CNN. Deep Convolutional Generative Adversarial Network (DCGAN), represents an improved version of the original framework of GAN. It uses neural networks (CNNs) for its generator and discriminator components. This approach was proposed and presented by Radford et al. [12], mainly targeting an enhancement of quality and improving speeds of convergence for the generated samples. In our architecture of DCGAN, some major parameters would come into play. Here, it is assumed that the channel count (nc) is 3, which means that the input images are in color. The size of the latent vector (nz) that goes into the generator was set to 100. For both the generator and discriminator, their feature maps are conﬁgured to have a depth of 32, namely ngf and ndf . With these values speciﬁed, both generator and discriminator models are deﬁned according to Tables 2 and 3 respectively, and thus affect the degree of complexity of the features they learn. These options balance the model capacity and computational efﬁciency, which makes it possible to generate high-quality images while guaranteeing stable training dynamics. The network learned to generate images of pistons, classiﬁed into three conditions: Normal, Defected 1, and Defected 2. To augment the dataset for each condition, 500 images were generated. An Adam optimizer was used with learning rates equal to 0.002 for the Generator and 0.0002 for the Discriminator. The beta parameter was set to about 0.5 and 0.999 for the Generator and Discriminator respectively. In each of the steps, we employed the Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) for the loss function while using constant noise vectors to test the output from the Generator. The training was done on a CUDA device to optimize for computation. From Fig. 3, it is clear that the samples generated are very close to the original samples and therefore this proves that the DCGAN is effective in generating the data sample.

We use a learning rate of 0.001 and 50 epochs. Training batches consist of 64 samples, while evaluation batches consist of 16 samples. The training process involves updating the model, in which the weights are progressively reﬁned using the backpropagation algorithm to optimize the loss function. This should ideally provide the best envi-ronment for the model to improve in regards to the classiﬁcation of images. During the training of the CNN model, the classiﬁcation ability of the model is evaluated by the use of precision, recall, and F1-score metrics. Precision is computed using Eq. (4). Precision =TP TP + FP (4) where TP (True Positive) means the samples that are correctly classiﬁed and FP (False Positives) means the samples that are misclassiﬁed. Recall is calculated according to Eq. (5). Recall = Sensitivity =TP TP + FN (5) where FN (False Negatives) is the samples that are being overlooked by the classiﬁer. The F1-Score is then obtained through Eq. (6). F1 Score = 2 × Precision × Recall Precision + Recall (6) In these formulas, precision indicates the percentage of TP (correctly classi-ﬁed positives) out of all predictions of the positive class, and recall evaluates the percentage of the model ﬁnding all positives in the test set. The F1-score is chosen because it gives an assessment of how the classiﬁer performs overall.
